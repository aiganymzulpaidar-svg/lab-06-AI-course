# REPORT — what the measurement showed, who it affects, and whose responsibility it is

Every number below was printed by the submitted run of `lab06_harness_says_no.ipynb` (model Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, greedy decoding, prompt v1, 40 pairs per group, SEED 2026, clean profiles). Statements marked as judgement are my reading of those numbers, not something the notebook printed.

## 1. Measurement read-out

The bare agent decided on 200 matched pairs, 400 profiles in all: 40 pairs each for name_language, gender_coded_name, university and for the two floors, null_name and null_university. It shortlisted 356 of 400 profiles (89.0 percent) and returned 0 unparseable replies. A flip is a pair whose two members got different outcomes. name_language flipped in 2 of 40 pairs (5.0 percent, 95 percent CI 1.4 to 16.5), gender_coded_name in 4 of 40 (10.0 percent, 4.0 to 23.1) and university in 1 of 40 (2.5 percent, 0.4 to 12.9). The floors flipped in 0 of 40 (null_name, 0.0 percent, 0.0 to 8.8) and 1 of 40 (null_university, 2.5 percent, 0.4 to 12.9). Every real attribute interval overlaps the interval of its own floor, so none of the three has been shown to move the decision, and describing any of them as an effect on the decision would be an unsupported claim.

Direction and magnitude give two observations that stay below that standard. All 4 gender_coded_name flips went the same way: the female-coded name was shortlisted and the male-coded one was not (0 against 4, exact McNemar p 0.125); female-coded profiles were shortlisted 90.0 percent of the time and male-coded profiles 80.0 percent. The score gap, male-coded minus female-coded, is -0.4 points [-1.2, +0.6] (sign test p 0.219). For name language the flips were 2 for Kazakh-language names against 0 (p 0.500), 0 against 1 for Russian and 0 against 1 for English, with score gaps inside about one point and intervals that span zero (Kazakh +0.7 [-1.8, +3.8], Russian +0.2 [-1.7, +2.1], English -1.0 [-4.2, +1.9]). University is the only attribute with a consistent score direction: flagship minus regional is +2.5 points [+1.1, +4.1], sign test p 0.002 (mean score 63.2 for flagship profiles, 60.8 for regional). The mean absolute score difference for university pairs is 2.50 over 10 pairs whose scores differ, against 2.12 over 9 pairs for null_university, so the size of the movement is close to that of an irrelevant change while its sign is not. I did not print a signed null_university gap and cannot say how far +2.5 stands above it. I report it as a score shift, not as a decision effect: with 89.0 percent of profiles already shortlisted, 2.5 points seldom crosses the threshold.

What 40 pairs could and could not detect: an exact two-sided McNemar test at 0.05 calls a result significant only from at least 6 one-sided pairs (15.0 percent of 40) when none go the other way, 8 when 1 does and 10 when 2 do. A real effect that moves fewer pairs than that cannot be told from chance in this run, so the absence of a measured effect is not a measured absence. The floors cover one thing only: another name of the same language group and gender coding, and another university of the same tier. They do not bound sensitivity to anything else, and the optional order probe shows how much that leaves out. In 40 pairs of identical applicants, where only the order of the profile lines differs, the agent gave a different decision in 10 pairs (25.0 percent, 14.2 to 40.2), always in the same direction (shortlisted in the default order and not in the other, 10 against 0, exact McNemar p 0.002), and the mean score in the other order was 6.6 points lower [-9.9, -3.5]. A change that carries no information about the applicant moves the decision more often than any name or university change I tested, and moves the score by more than the university gap. My reading is that the differences in the first table cannot be separated from the layout fragility of the prompt, and that the floors understate it. A stronger estimate needs several prompt wordings and layouts, a larger number of pairs near the decision threshold, and a second model. My prediction was also wrong in size: I expected university to flip 25 to 35 percent of pairs and the floors to sit at 5 to 15 percent, and measured 2.5 percent and 0.0 to 2.5 percent.

The run supports nothing about another prompt, another model or real applicants. It used one prompt, one 3-billion-parameter model, synthetic names written in Latin script from three language groups, and fictional universities in two tiers.

## 2. Who is affected

A wrong decision has two sides. The wrongly rejected applicant is the one who sits close to the threshold on the wrong side of it. In the bare run the 40 to 49 score row is the only one that holds both rejects (33) and shortlists (31); the highest reject score and the lowest shortlist score are both 45. Applicants in that row decide the outcome of the screening, and the score shifts I measured are the size that moves someone across it: +2.5 points for a flagship university against a regional one, 6.6 points lower on average for the same applicant when the profile lines are reordered, and a mean absolute change of 14.01 points over the 166 of 400 profiles whose score changed when name and university were hidden. The group that carries the measured university shift is the regional-university applicants (mean score 60.8 against 63.2, shortlisted 90.0 percent against 92.5 percent). The group behind the 4-against-0 direction is male-coded applicants (shortlisted 80.0 percent against 90.0 percent). Neither count is significant, so these are the groups to watch, not groups shown to be harmed. The wrongly shortlisted applicant is the one who takes a place from someone else. At this stage the pool is not scarce for the model, which shortlists 89.0 percent; the scarcity arrives at the award stage, and the brief does not say how many places exist, so the number of people displaced is unknown to me.

The cost differs by side. A wrongly rejected applicant loses the scholarship, time spent on the application and, in this system, receives a recorded rejection from a model without anyone having read the file; the harness reduces that risk only for the 34 of 400 profiles it sends to a person. A wrongly shortlisted applicant costs the fund reviewer time and displaces a better applicant later. Other parties are affected: the reviewers, who read 34 of 400 profiles (8.5 percent) and bear the workload that sets the quality of the whole control; people who never applied because they expected a rejection, whom nothing in this run measures; applicants from institutions the model has never seen, who are now judged without the university field; minors and applicants who did not consent, whom the harness refuses with no decision (1 of each among the six test requests); and applicants whose input is malformed, who are refused as invalid. If a divergence exists that n = 40 could not detect, the cost falls on the groups named above, and the detection limit of 6, 8 and 10 pairs says how large it would have to be to be seen.

The consequences compound over years. A score gap of 2.5 points between flagship and regional universities that is passed to a later review, another fund or a published ranking is copied into each of them, and the fund that reuses cut-offs fixed on one year's scores inherits the calibration problem I measured: the band planned at 16.0 percent of profiles (64 of 400) sent 8.5 percent (34 of 400) once the prompt changed.

## 3. Professional responsibility

As the developer of the harness I am responsible for the following before it runs. I must recalibrate the score band on the blinded scores, because the band 40 to 49 planned at 16.0 percent sent 8.5 percent, and the blinded prompt changed the score of 166 of 400 profiles. I must freeze and version the prompt layout, because layout alone flipped 25.0 percent of identical pairs; the log carries the prompt version for that reason. I must tell the people who deploy it what the numbers allow: no divergence above its floor was measured, n = 40 could not detect an effect that moves fewer than 6 to 10 pairs, blinding changed the outcome of 46 of 400 profiles (12 of them between shortlist and reject), and the harness removes name and university from the model's input without making the decisions fair. I must not claim that the zero flips after the harness show that the model is unbiased; they are zero by construction because the two members of those pairs are shown identical text. I must not claim that programme or household income are free of divergence: I did not test them (age was tested in Part 6, section 4).

Discharging each of these takes a concrete act. For the band, a rerun of the blinded measurement and a new choice from its score table. For the prompt, a stop condition: no change of wording or layout without repeating the order probe and the matched-pair run. For the disclosure, this report and `JUSTIFICATION.md` handed to the deployer. What I did in the lab: measured the bare agent, built five controls (input validation, blinding of name and university, refusal, a decision log, escalation), exercised the refusal path (5 of 6 requests refused with no model call, 0 of 400 clean applicants refused), produced a log of 406 records, and ran two optional probes: the order probe above and a counterfactual check on 8 name-language pairs (16 profiles, 32 model calls, 2 escalated as borderline, 0 counterfactual disagreements). What is left: testing programme and income, more pairs, other prompts and models, a test of the refusal rules on real names, and the parts only the fund can do: sending the applicant notices, staffing the review queue, answering objections and keeping the head hash of the log outside the file.

The institution owns the purpose of the system, the choice of who reviews the escalated cases, the handling of objections and appeals, the information given to applicants and the retention of the log. I own the correctness of the controls and the honesty of what I report about them. The two conflict where the institution wants a control removed to save time or money. If the fund asks to remove escalation because reading 34 of 400 profiles costs reviewer hours, I have measured what the control carries: 34 profiles the model would decide alone, 22 of which the bare agent rejected. I would give the fund those numbers, propose a narrower band and a cap on review time in place of removal, and refuse to certify the system as reviewed by a person if no person reads the escalated cases. If the fund asks to remove blinding, I would restate the university score gap of +2.5 points (sign test p 0.002) and the 4-against-0 direction, state that the counts do not reach significance, and leave the decision to the fund in writing.

## 4. What Part 6 added after the first run

Age was visible to the model and untested. In 40 pairs of identical applicants that differ only in age (19 against 25, name and university blinded), the model's decision flipped in 1 pair (2.5%, 95 percent CI 0.4% to 12.9%): the younger applicant was shortlisted alone in 1 pair and the older one alone in 0 (exact McNemar p 1.000). The score, older minus younger, moved by -1.75 points on average; 4 of 40 pairs differ and the mean absolute difference is 1.75. Floors from Part 1: null_name 0 of 40 (0.0%, 95 percent CI 0.0% to 8.8%); null_university 1 of 40 (2.5%, 95 percent CI 0.4% to 12.9%); layout alone flipped 10 of 40 identical pairs. The age flip interval overlaps the interval of at least one null floor, so age has not been shown to move the decision in this run; that is not a measured absence (n = 40 detects only the pair counts listed in section 1). The age pairs reuse measured profiles, so they share the 3-billion-parameter model, the single prompt and the synthetic population of everything above.

The band was chosen on the scores of the bare agent. On the blinded scores read back from the log (400 profiles) the table is: score 20-29: 24 rejects, 0 shortlists; score 40-49: 34 rejects, 0 shortlists; score 50-59: 0 rejects, 4 shortlists; score 60-69: 0 rejects, 76 shortlists; score 70-79: 0 rejects, 238 shortlists; score 80-89: 0 rejects, 24 shortlists. The planned band (40, 49) holds 34 of 400 blinded scores (8.5%). No score row holds both rejects and shortlists: every reject of the blinded model is in rows up to 40-49 and every shortlist in rows from 50-59, so the rule of Part 2d finds nothing and I take the two rows next to the cut, (40, 59). That band holds 38 (9.5%). Replaying the logged model decisions, the planned band gives {'shortlist': 342, 'reject': 24, 'escalate': 34} and the recalibrated band {'shortlist': 338, 'reject': 24, 'escalate': 38}. The planned band covers only the reject side of the cut when no shortlist falls in it; the recalibrated band also sends to a person the shortlists nearest the cut. The submitted decisions.jsonl keeps the planned band; the replay is a calculation on recorded replies, not a new run.

The two own escalation rules, which fired 0 times in the measured run, were exercised with scripted model replies: shortlist with a score of 30 gave escalate (shortlist_with_low_score); reject with a score of 75 gave escalate (reject_with_high_score); reason names a prestige cue gave escalate (reason_cites_protected_attribute); an ordinary shortlist gave shortlist (model_decision). That shows that the rules work as written; it does not show that they catch real model errors, which the measured run never produced.
