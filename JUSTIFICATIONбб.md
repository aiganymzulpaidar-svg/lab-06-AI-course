# JUSTIFICATION — the design of the harness, and why

Everything described here is in the notebook code or in the submitted run (log of 406 records in `decisions.jsonl`, register in `control_register.json`). The Kazakh statutes were checked in a second pass against their text on `zakon.uchet.kz`, a mirror of the Adilet text (`adilet.zan.kz` returns no text without JavaScript); that mirror may be later than the official text, and Law 230-VIII was amended by Law No. 326-VIII of 24 June 2026 in Articles 1, 13, 17, 20 and 28, whose new wording I could not read, so Article 17 in particular has to be compared with the official text. The EU Regulations and the OECD Recommendation were read in the first session on `eur-lex.europa.eu` and `legalinstruments.oecd.org` and were not rechecked in the second. The UNESCO Recommendation is cited by principle name and section (III.2, Principles), with no paragraph numbers, because I could not read it page by page. The student then opened the cited provisions in the primary texts (the two Kazakh statutes on `adilet.zan.kz`, the EU Regulations on `eur-lex.europa.eu` and the OECD Recommendation on the OECD site) and found that they match what is cited here; nothing here is cited on the strength of a statement by an AI model alone (see the declaration).

## 1. What the system may do

The system screens applications to the Tulpar Scholarship Fund (fictional). It reads one profile of merit and need fields and returns shortlist or reject, a score from 0 to 100 and a one-sentence reason. It may decide on its own only when four conditions hold: the request has the purpose scholarship_screening, the applicant is 18 or older and has consented to automated processing, every field passes the type and range check and the free-text fields pass my text rule, and the model's score lies outside the band 40 to 49. Every other case is refused or sent to a person. The system informs the award stage; it does not award. Under Article 17(1) of Law 230-VIII the owner classifies the system; I classify it as medium risk (a wrong decision causes moral harm or material damage to an applicant) and as medium autonomy under Article 17(2)(2), which keeps a person able to correct or cancel the decision. That classification is my judgement and the fund would have to confirm it.

Each control sits in one place in the notebook. Blinding is `BLIND = ("name", "university")` in Part 2a and is applied by `screen` through `Harness(blind=...)`. The refusal rule is `my_extra_refusal` in Part 2b, next to the built-in checks `validate_profile` and `refusal_reason` of the supplied code. The escalation rule is `my_extra_escalation` in Part 2c and the band `BORDERLINE = (40, 49)` in Part 2d. The log is `log_path="decisions.jsonl"` in the same `Harness(...)` call, which switches on five controls: validate_input, blind, refuse, log and escalate. The counterfactual check is off in the submitted harness and was run only as an extension.

Constraints changed the design in three places. Article 6 of Law 230-VIII (no discrimination by gender, nationality, language or any other circumstance) made me remove name and university from the model's input instead of keeping them and monitoring the output; without it I would have kept the bare prompt, because Part 1 showed no flip rate above its floor. Article 19-1(1) of Law 94-V (automated processing that changes rights is prohibited without consent) made consent a hard refusal and not a field the model reads. Article 17(2)(2) made me route a score band to a person instead of letting the model decide every case; without it the system would have been the bare pipeline, which shortlisted 356 of 400 profiles and sent none to anyone.

## 2. What it must refuse

It refuses five kinds of request, and each refusal is enforced in code and fired in the submitted run (Part 4, six requests, 5 refused, 0 model calls for each refusal, one record per refusal in the log). A purpose other than scholarship_screening is refused as purpose_not_allowed (`refusal_reason`; fired for the request to rank applicants by ethnicity). An applicant under 18 is refused as minor (fired for the 17-year-old). An applicant without consent is refused as no_consent (fired). Impossible values are refused as invalid_input by `validate_profile` (fired for GPA 9.9 and exam score -5). A name, university or programme that holds instruction-like words, or a name that is not letters joined by space, apostrophe, hyphen or full stop, is refused by my own rule with a code such as instruction_like_text_in_name (fired for the name that was an instruction).

Each refusal also blocks a legitimate case. The minor rule blocks a 17-year-old who may apply with a legal representative, and the no_consent rule blocks an adult who left a box unticked. The text rule blocks a real surname that is also one of the listed words (for example Prompt or Reply) and any name containing a digit or a character outside the allowed set. The applicant bears each cost: the place is lost without a person reading the file. I accept the cost because each refusal is recorded with a code, and I treat 0 refusals of the 400 clean applicants in the measured pairs as a lower bound on false refusals in a synthetic population, not as an estimate for real applicants. After a refusal, Part 6 builds a notice for the applicant from the log record (`applicant_notices.jsonl`: the reason in plain words, the right to object and the three-working-day answer period) and a way to record an objection with its due date (`file_objection`). Nothing in the notebook sends a notice or staffs the review: that remains with the fund (section 6).

## 3. What record it keeps

The audit question is: for application P, which fields did the model see, what did it answer, which control stopped or redirected the request, and who has to look, so that someone else can reconstruct the outcome without re-running the model? Each line of `decisions.jsonl` answers it with the keys record_id, pid, input_sha256, fields_shown, fields_withheld, model_id, prompt_version, harness, raw_output, decision, score, controls_fired, outcome, reason_code, escalated_to_human and timestamp. The file holds 406 records: 400 from the measured run and 6 from Part 4. By outcome they are shortlist 343, reject 24, escalate 34 and refuse 5; by reason code model_decision 367, borderline 34 and one each of purpose_not_allowed, minor, no_consent, invalid_input and instruction_like_text_in_name; 34 records have escalated_to_human set, and the most common set of withheld fields is name and university.

The record leaves out the values of the applicant's fields on purpose. It holds field names and the SHA-256 of the exact text the model saw, so the log is not a second copy of the applicant data; `raw_output` is the model's reply and can quote a value the model was shown (a blinded field is never shown). The cost of that line is that the log cannot rebuild the input text; a reviewer needs the application store and the hash to prove which text was judged. The notebook does not implement retention or access control. Protection against later edits is partial: Part 6 seals the log with a hash chain (`decisions.jsonl.chain`, each entry committing to the line before it, so an edited, removed or reordered record is detected at its line), but a person with write access can rebuild the whole chain, so the head hash only protects the log if it is kept outside the file; section 3a gives it. I found no provision to cite for a retention period and set none; the fund has to set one that covers the period in which an applicant can object (Article 19-1(3) of Law 94-V gives three working days to consider an objection).

## 4. The legal constraints

The fund and the data are fictional, so no instrument binds the prototype. If the fund operated in Kazakhstan, where this course runs, two statutes apply, and I use them as the design basis. Law of the Republic of Kazakhstan No. 230-VIII of 17 November 2025 "On Artificial Intelligence" (Article 31: in force after sixty calendar days from its first official publication on 18 November 2025, that is from 18 January 2026 as I count it) applies to a system that makes or supports decisions about people. Article 6 requires systems to be created and operated in a way that ensures fairness and equality and excludes any discrimination on grounds of origin, social, official and property status and the further grounds the article lists. Article 7(2) gives the person affected by an AI decision the right to be informed about the procedure of automated processing, its consequences and the possibility to object. Article 8(1) obliges owner, possessor or user to keep continuous control over the system. Article 16(1)(4)-(5) gives users the right to explanations of results that affect them and to information about the data on which a decision was made, to the extent set by the user agreement. Article 17 divides systems by risk (paragraph 1) and by autonomy (paragraph 2). Article 18(1) defines risk management over the life cycle, including foreseeable misuse. Law No. 94-V of 21 May 2013 "On Personal Data and their Protection" applies because the fund collects and processes applicant data: Article 7(1) requires consent of the person or the legal representative; Article 19-1(1) prohibits automated processing that gives, changes or ends a person's rights unless the subject consented or a law provides otherwise; Article 19-1(2)-(3) requires the owner or operator to explain the procedure, allow an objection and consider it within three working days; Article 22(1) requires protective measures against unauthorised access.

The law requires outcomes and rights, and leaves the means to me. Article 6 does not say to blind a field, Article 16 does not prescribe a log format, and Article 18 does not prescribe a range check. Blinding, the log schema and the validation are therefore my choices, justified by those articles and by Part 1; the band 40 to 49 and the extra rules are mine as well. I did more than the law requires in logging the withheld fields and the prompt version, and in testing prompt-layout sensitivity (the order probe), which no cited provision asks for.

Two EU instruments are benchmarks only, because nothing places the fund or its applicants in the EU. Regulation (EU) 2016/679 Article 22(1) gives the person the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing that produces legal or similarly significant effects, paragraph 2(c) allows it on explicit consent, and paragraph 3 requires at least the right to obtain human intervention, to express a view and to contest the decision. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 lists in Annex III point 3(a) AI systems intended to determine access or admission to educational institutions at all levels as high-risk; whether a scholarship shortlist is "access" is a question I do not settle. If it is, Article 12(1) requires automatic event logs and Article 14(1)-(2) requires that the system can be effectively overseen by natural persons to prevent or minimise risks to fundamental rights, and Article 10(2)(f)-(g) requires examination of the training, validation and testing data for possible biases and measures to detect, prevent and mitigate them (it concerns the data, not the outputs of a deployed model).

The texts that speak to automated decisions specifically are Article 19-1 of Law 94-V, Article 7(2) and Article 17(2) of Law 230-VIII, and Article 22 of the GDPR. Two controls have no provision that names them. validate_input rests on the general duty of risk management in Article 18(1) of Law 230-VIII and on no rule that asks for range checks; blind rests on Article 6, which states the outcome and not the means. These two were the hardest to tie to a provision. I searched before writing this file, so I cannot present that as a prediction made beforehand.

## 5. The ethical principles

The sources are the OECD Recommendation on Artificial Intelligence (OECD/LEGAL/0449, adopted 22 May 2019, amended 3 May 2024) and the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (adopted 23 November 2021). Neither is law. The OECD text is a Recommendation, a non-binding instrument; the UNESCO text recommends that Member States apply its provisions on a voluntary basis. Four principles bear on four decisions in this system. Fairness and non-discrimination (OECD principle 1.2(a); UNESCO section III.2, principle Fairness and non-discrimination) bears on which fields the model sees, so on blinding. Human oversight and determination (OECD 1.2(b); UNESCO section III.2, principle Human oversight and determination) bears on who decides the cases in the band 40 to 49 and the unparseable replies, so on escalation. Privacy and data protection (OECD 1.2(a); UNESCO section III.2, principle Right to privacy and data protection) bears on consent, age and what the log stores. Transparency and explainability (OECD 1.3, including the means to challenge an output; UNESCO section III.2, principle Transparency and explainability) and accountability and traceability (OECD 1.5(b); UNESCO section III.2, principle Responsibility and accountability) bear on what the log keeps and on what the applicant is told, and robustness (OECD 1.4) bears on the validation of inputs.

Principles conflict in this design. Treating applicants identically conflicts with treating them fairly: blinding the university gives every applicant the same view of the others and removes information about preparation that a fund could value, and I put the removal of a measured score channel first because the fund cannot audit what a 3-billion-parameter model infers from a university name. Privacy conflicts with accountability: a log that stores no field values cannot rebuild the input without the application store, and I chose the hash over the copy. Oversight conflicts with the reviewers' time: the band sends 34 of 400 profiles to a person, and a wider band would protect more applicants and cost more reading. Ethics demands one thing the law as I read it does not: because the agent's decision flipped in 10 of 40 pairs of identical applicants when only the line order changed, the fairness principle requires me to treat prompt stability as part of the measurement and to say that the floors do not cover it; no cited provision asks for that.

## 6. The trade-offs

Each control has a price and a harm it prevents. Blinding cost no model calls (400 for 400 profiles in both runs; prompt tokens 84409 against 77922; 479.7 seconds against 479.6) and changed the outcome of 46 of 400 profiles, 12 of them between shortlist and reject, and the score of 166 of 400 by a mean absolute 14.01 points; it prevents the university score gap of +2.5 points (sign test p 0.002) and the 4-against-0 gender direction from reaching the model. Validation and refusal cost no model calls and refuse 5 of 6 test requests and 0 of 400 clean applicants; they prevent decisions on data the system must not process. The log costs storage and a privacy line (no values stored). Escalation costs reviewer time: 34 of 400 profiles (8.5 percent), all with the reason code borderline; my two extra escalation rules fired 0 times.

The band is a trade-off too, and it did not transfer. I planned 16.0 percent (64 of 400 baseline profiles in the 40 to 49 row) and the harness sent 8.5 percent (34 of 400), because the blinded prompt shifted the scores. The submitted run keeps the planned band; Part 6 recomputes the band from the blinded scores in the log and replays the logged decisions under it (section 3a), which is a calculation on recorded replies and not a new run. The cases outside the band, among them 24 rejects, are decided by the model and nobody re-checks them. I chose the balance because a reviewer team can read 34 profiles and because the band covers the only score row in which the bare agent produced both rejects and shortlists. I would widen the band to the 50 to 59 row (46 baseline shortlists and no rejects) if reviewer capacity allowed, and I would turn the counterfactual check on if the fund could pay four model calls per pair in place of one (the extension on 8 name-language pairs used 32 calls and produced 0 disagreements, 2 borderline escalations).

The design still fails to protect against the following, and I did not close these gaps. The agent changes its decision with the layout of the prompt (10 of 40 identical pairs, 25.0 percent, mean score 6.6 points lower in the other order); freezing the layout does not make the agent stable, and a fix would take an ensemble or a canonical rendering and a rerun of every measurement. Age was tested in Part 6 (REPORT.md section 4); programme and household income stay visible and were not tested for divergence (income is a need criterion and programme a merit criterion, so a difference there may be legitimate and needs a different design). The text rule works by keywords: the name and university fields are blinded, so an instruction in them cannot reach the model, but the programme field is shown to the model and is protected by the same keyword list only. Notices and the objection record exist as code and files, but nobody sends the notices, staffs the review queue or answers objections, although Article 7(2) of Law 230-VIII and Article 19-1(2)-(3) of Law 94-V require the information and the answer. The hash chain does not stop someone with write access from rebuilding both files. The measurement used one 3-billion-parameter model, one prompt, synthetic names and fictional universities, and 40 pairs per group.

### 3a. The integrity of the record and what the applicant is told

After the run, Part 6 seals the log: `decisions.jsonl` holds 406 records and `decisions.jsonl.chain` one chain entry per record (SHA-256 of the previous chain value and of the line). Head hash: `2d456c0e4590e220ef59666e8eae5892feabddd2030d6ac5b4e647fbd2e881b2`. Verification of the file against its chain and this head hash: True. A copy of the log in which one escalation was rewritten as a shortlist fails verification at line 17. The head hash protects the log only if it is stored outside it (here it is in this file, which is in the repository with the log, so a person who can rewrite both is not stopped; the fund should keep it in a separate system).

`review_queue.jsonl` lists the 34 escalated cases (status open, no reviewer, no human decision) and `applicant_notices.jsonl` holds 406 notices, one per record, each stating the outcome or the reason for the refusal, that the decision was automated, the right to object and the answer period of 3 working days. `file_objection` records an objection with its due date (an objection received on Friday 20 November 2026 is due 2026-11-25; public holidays are not modelled). These files are derived from the log and prove that the information can be produced from it; they do not prove that anyone sent it.

The band: The planned band (40, 49) holds 34 of 400 blinded scores (8.5%). No score row holds both rejects and shortlists: every reject of the blinded model is in rows up to 40-49 and every shortlist in rows from 50-59, so the rule of Part 2d finds nothing and I take the two rows next to the cut, (40, 59). That band holds 38 (9.5%). Replaying the logged model decisions, the planned band gives {'shortlist': 342, 'reject': 24, 'escalate': 34} and the recalibrated band {'shortlist': 338, 'reject': 24, 'escalate': 38}. The planned band covers only the reject side of the cut when no shortlist falls in it; the recalibrated band also sends to a person the shortlists nearest the cut. The submitted decisions.jsonl keeps the planned band; the replay is a calculation on recorded replies, not a new run.
